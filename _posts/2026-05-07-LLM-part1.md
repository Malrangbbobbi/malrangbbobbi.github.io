## 0. Why Sequences Are Hard

Consider two versions of the same earnings announcement.

> "Revenue missed expectations. Earnings beat."

> "Earnings beat. Revenue missed expectations."

Same words. Different story. The first framing leads with disappointment and rescues itself — the market will likely focus on the miss. The second leads with the win and softens the blow — analysts will probably give the company the benefit of the doubt. A model that treats both sentences as identical inputs — because they contain exactly the same words — is guaranteed to make systematically wrong predictions.

This is the defining challenge of sequential data: **order is meaning**. A price series of [100, 105, 103, 108] tells a fundamentally different story than [108, 103, 105, 100], even though both contain the same four values. The trajectory encodes momentum, volatility, and trend — information that evaporates the moment you treat the data as a set rather than a sequence.

Standard feedforward networks have no mechanism to handle this. They take a fixed-size input, transform it through a series of layers, and produce a fixed-size output — there's no memory, no notion of what came before. For sequential data, you need something that can accumulate context over time.

## 1. RNN: The First Attempt

The Recurrent Neural Network answered this need with an elegantly simple idea: give the network a feedback loop. At each time step $t$, the model takes two inputs — the current observation $x_t$ and a summary of everything seen so far, encoded as a hidden state $h_{t-1}$ — and produces an updated hidden state

$$h_t = \tanh(W_h h_{t-1} + W_x x_t + b)$$

That hidden state then carries forward to the next step, acting as the network's working memory. Unrolled across a sequence, an RNN looks like a chain of identical networks sharing the same weights, each passing its state to the next.

For a trading signal model reading news headlines word by word, the hidden state after reading "Revenue" should encode something like *we're in an earnings context*. ### The Vanishing Gradient Problem

In theory. In practice, a serious problem hides inside backpropagation.

When we train the network, we compute how much each parameter contributed to the final loss. For a sequence of length $T$, the gradient of the loss with respect to an early hidden state $h_1$ involves a chain of partial derivatives — one per step

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h_1} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h_T} \cdot \prod_{t=2}^{T} \frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-1}}$$

Each factor involves the weight matrix $W_h$ and the derivative of $\tanh$, which is bounded between 0 and 1. If $\|W_h\| < 1$, this product shrinks exponentially with sequence length

$$\left\|\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h_1}\right\| \approx \|W_h\|^{T-1} \cdot \left\|\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h_T}\right\|$$

For a sequence of length 50, even $\|W_h\| = 0.9$ gives $0.9^{49} \approx 0.005$. The gradient signal reaching $h_1$ is essentially zero — the model learns almost nothing from its earliest inputs.

In practice, an RNN reading a lengthy earnings call transcript will learn to predict the next word almost entirely from the last few tokens. The "memory" it supposedly carries is, in reality, extremely short-term. For financial NLP tasks where guidance given in the opening remarks heavily influences interpretation of later questions, this is a fundamental failure.

One thing that confused me early on was whether "vanishing" gradient means the gradient becomes exactly zero. It doesn't — it becomes exponentially small, which means parameter updates for early time steps are negligible compared to those for recent steps. The model still trains. It just effectively ignores anything more than a few steps back.

## 2. LSTM: Remembering What Matters

The LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) was designed specifically to fix the vanishing gradient problem. Its central insight was to separate short-term working memory — the hidden state $h_t$ — from long-term storage — a cell state $C_t$ — and to give the network explicit mechanisms called gates to control what gets written to and read from that long-term storage.

The **forget gate** decides what fraction of the previous cell state to retain

$$f_t = \sigma(W_f \cdot [h_{t-1},\, x_t] + b_f)$$

The **input gate** and candidate decide what new information to write

$$i_t = \sigma(W_i \cdot [h_{t-1},\, x_t] + b_i), \qquad \tilde{C}_t = \tanh(W_C \cdot [h_{t-1},\, x_t] + b_C)$$

The **cell state** updates as a combination of forgetting and adding

$$C_t = f_t \odot C_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde{C}_t$$

The **output gate** filters which part of the cell state to expose as the hidden state

$$o_t = \sigma(W_o \cdot [h_{t-1},\, x_t] + b_o), \qquad h_t = o_t \odot \tanh(C_t)$$

The key to LSTM's gradient stability is the additive cell state update

$$C_t = f_t \odot C_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde{C}_t$$

Information from 30 steps ago can flow through $C_t$ largely intact, because the gradient path back through additive operations is far gentler than through a chain of nonlinear activations. The forget gate can be set close to 1 — remember everything — and the gradient flows without exponential decay.

For a model reading a quarterly earnings call, this matters enormously. When an analyst raises a follow-up question in minute 45 that references revenue guidance given in minute 3, the LSTM has a mechanism to hold that guidance in the cell state until it becomes relevant.

One thing that confused me was why LSTM needs both a cell state $C_t$ and a hidden state $h_t$. Think of $C_t$ as a long-term scratchpad that accumulates context over the whole sequence, and $h_t$ as the filtered view of that scratchpad that gets exposed as output at each step. The cell state is optimized for stable gradient flow; the hidden state is optimized for expressiveness at the current time step. Both are necessary.

### What LSTM Still Doesn't Fix

LSTM substantially improved long-range memory. But it left two structural problems untouched.

The first is **sequential dependency**. Every step must wait for the previous one — $h_t$ cannot be computed until $h_{t-1}$ is ready. Processing a sequence of length $T$ requires $T$ sequential passes, regardless of hardware. GPU parallelism is wasted. On long documents, this is cripplingly slow.

The second is the **context vector bottleneck** — a problem that would become visible only when LSTM was embedded inside a larger architecture meant for sequence-to-sequence tasks.

## 3. Seq2Seq: The Encoder-Decoder Idea

By 2014, the NLP community wanted to go beyond predicting the next token. They wanted to transform one sequence into another — translate a sentence, summarize a paragraph, generate a report from structured data. These tasks share a common structure: variable-length input, variable-length output, lengths that don't have to match.

Seq2Seq addressed this with two LSTMs. An **encoder** reads the entire input sequence and compresses it into a single vector — the context vector $c$, taken as the encoder's final hidden state

$$c = h_T^{enc}$$

A **decoder** then takes $c$ as its initial state and generates the output sequence autoregressively — one token at a time, each output feeding into the next step

$$h_t^{dec} = f(h_{t-1}^{dec},\, y_{t-1},\, c)$$

For a model trained to translate financial news from English to French, or to generate a structured summary from an earnings transcript, this architecture is a natural fit. The encoder and decoder are entirely separate networks — different weight matrices, different learning objectives — trained jointly. The encoder learns to compress meaning; the decoder learns to expand it.

One thing that confused me when first reading about this was whether the encoder and decoder share their weights. They don't. Tying the weights would force both networks to simultaneously be good at compression and generation — an unnecessarily hard constraint. In practice, they're always kept separate.

## 4. The Breaking Point

Seq2Seq worked remarkably well for short sequences. On translation benchmarks with sentences under 20 words, it matched or exceeded previous state-of-the-art approaches. But there was a fundamental problem baked into the design that no engineering could fix.

The encoder compresses an arbitrarily long input into a **fixed-size** context vector. A five-word sentence and a five-thousand-word document both have to fit into the same $d$-dimensional vector. As input grows longer, information inevitably gets discarded.

For a model summarizing SEC filings, this creates a systematic failure mode. The Q1 revenue guidance given in section 2 is unlikely to survive compression when the executive compensation tables in section 14 are still fresh in the encoder's memory. The summaries that emerge reflect the end of the document far more than the beginning — not because the model is wrong, but because the architecture physically cannot preserve all the context.

This showed up clearly in the numbers. BLEU scores on translation tasks dropped sharply once sentence length exceeded 30 words. The degradation wasn't caused by insufficient training data or model capacity — it was caused by the architecture itself. There was no version of this design that could scale to long documents.

The field needed a model that could look back at any part of the input directly — not through a single compressed vector, but through direct access, weighted by relevance. The context vector bottleneck wasn't an implementation detail to engineer around. It was the core assumption that needed to be abandoned.
