## 0. The Decoder-Only Bet

The original 2017 Transformer was designed for translation — a task with a clear input sequence and a clear output sequence in a different language. The encoder read the source, the decoder generated the target, and cross-attention connected them. For translation, this makes perfect sense.

But most language tasks aren't translation. Sentiment classification, question answering, summarization, text completion — all of these take text in and produce some response. They don't require two separate sequences in fundamentally different languages. They require a model that can read a context and continue it intelligently.

GPT made a simple observation: if your goal is to generate text conditioned on prior context, you only need the decoder half of the Transformer.

Removing the encoder and cross-attention does more than simplify the architecture. It eliminates the need for paired training data — the aligned source-target pairs required to train a translation model. Instead, GPT can be trained on raw text alone: any book, article, or document works. The training signal is simply predicting the next token, which is self-supervised — the labels come from the data itself.

This was the bet. Massive amounts of unlabeled text exist. If you can extract useful representations just by predicting what comes next, you can scale training data far beyond what any labeled dataset could provide.

## 1. GPT-1: The Pre-training Hypothesis

GPT-1, released by OpenAI in 2018, tested a specific hypothesis: pre-train a large language model on raw text, then fine-tune it on small labeled datasets for specific tasks. The hypothesis was that the pre-training phase would force the model to build rich internal representations of language — representations general enough to be adapted to downstream tasks with minimal labeled data.

Pre-training maximizes the likelihood of each token given the previous $k$ tokens

$$L_1(\mathcal{U}) = \sum_i \log P(u_i \mid u_{i-k}, \ldots, u_{i-1};\, \Theta)$$

This is standard language modeling, nothing novel. What GPT-1 added was the two-stage training protocol. After pre-training on BooksCorpus (800M words), the model was fine-tuned on labeled datasets for specific tasks by adding a task-specific output layer and optimizing a supervised objective

$$L_2(\mathcal{C}) = \sum_{(x,y)} \log P(y \mid x^1, \ldots, x^m)$$

But rather than discarding the language modeling objective during fine-tuning, GPT-1 kept it as an auxiliary loss

$$L_3(\mathcal{C}) = L_2(\mathcal{C}) + \lambda \cdot L_1(\mathcal{C})$$

For a quantitative hedge fund, this two-stage protocol maps naturally onto a real workflow. Pre-train once on all available financial text — 10-K filings, earnings transcripts, analyst reports — to build general financial language representations. Then fine-tune separately on small labeled datasets for each downstream task: sentiment classification for earnings calls, named entity recognition for news, binary classification for trade signal generation. The expensive pre-training is amortized across all downstream tasks.

One thing that confused me was the purpose of keeping $L_1$ during fine-tuning. The intuition is catastrophic forgetting — if you optimize only the task-specific objective $L_2$ on a small labeled dataset, the model rapidly overwrites its pre-trained representations to minimize the fine-tuning loss. Including $L_1$ as a regularizer forces the model to remain a good language model throughout, which preserves the general representations that make transfer learning work in the first place.

GPT-1's results validated the hypothesis: 117M parameters, trained on raw text, then fine-tuned with small amounts of labeled data, achieved state-of-the-art on 9 out of 12 NLP benchmarks in 2018. The pre-training paradigm worked.

## 2. GPT-2: Fine-tuning Is a Crutch

GPT-1's two-stage protocol had an implicit assumption: you need a labeled fine-tuning dataset for every task you care about. For financial NLP, that means separately curating labeled examples for sentiment analysis, event extraction, risk classification, and so on. This is expensive, slow, and fragile — small labeled datasets can overfit, and the label distributions may not generalize.

GPT-2 (2019, 1.5B parameters) pushed the hypothesis further: what if fine-tuning is unnecessary? What if a sufficiently large language model, trained on sufficiently diverse text, already knows how to do most tasks — and you just need to phrase the input correctly?

The theoretical grounding is a product rule decomposition. Any joint distribution over sequences of symbols factors as

$$p(x) = \prod_{n=1}^{N} p(s_n \mid s_1, \ldots, s_{n-1})$$

Every NLP task can be framed as a conditional text generation problem — predicting an output sequence given an input sequence — and language modeling is exactly that. Translation is $p(\text{French} \mid \text{English})$. Summarization is $p(\text{summary} \mid \text{document})$. Question answering is $p(\text{answer} \mid \text{document, question})$. If the model is powerful enough and has seen enough diverse text, all of these conditional distributions are captured within a single language model.

GPT-2's zero-shot performance was inconsistent. On some tasks it was impressive; on others it was weak. But the research contribution wasn't the benchmark numbers — it was the conceptual reframing. Tasks don't require separate models. Tasks don't require labeled data. They require the right prompt. This idea would become central to everything that followed.

GPT-2 also came with a now-famous decision: OpenAI declined to release the full model at launch, citing concerns about misuse for automated disinformation. Looking back, the concern was directionally correct — the model could generate coherent, convincing text at scale. Whether withholding a 2019 model meaningfully delayed that risk is a separate debate.

## 3. GPT-3: Few-Shot Learners

GPT-3 (2020, 175B parameters) scaled GPT-2's approach by roughly two orders of magnitude and introduced a more systematic study of what large language models can do without any weight updates at all — purely through examples provided in the prompt.

The key distinction is between three evaluation modes. **Zero-shot**: the model sees only a task description, no examples. **One-shot**: the model sees one example input-output pair before the test query. **Few-shot**: the model sees several examples. In all three cases, the model's weights are frozen — no gradient updates, no fine-tuning, no task-specific training.

  Figure 4. Zero-shot, one-shot, and few-shot prompting on a financial signal classification task. No model weights change between examples — the model adapts entirely through the context window.
</figcaption>
</figure>

GPT-3's central empirical finding was that few-shot performance scales reliably with model size. Smaller models (1.3B, 6.7B parameters) show minimal improvement from adding in-context examples. GPT-3 at 175B shows dramatic improvement — each additional example meaningfully narrows the gap to fine-tuned models.

This relationship between scale and capability follows what later became known as **scaling laws**. Kaplan et al. (2020) showed that language model loss decreases as a smooth power law in the number of parameters $N$, the amount of training data $D$, and compute budget $C$

$$L(N) \propto N^{-\alpha_N}, \quad L(D) \propto D^{-\alpha_D}$$

with exponents $\alpha_N \approx 0.076$ and $\alpha_D \approx 0.095$ empirically. The implication is that returns to scale are predictable — you can forecast roughly how much better a 10x larger model will be before training it. This turned scaling into a principled research strategy rather than an empirical gamble.

One thing that confused me was whether "emergent" capabilities in large models — abilities that appear abruptly above some parameter threshold — are genuinely discontinuous or a measurement artifact. The answer is probably both. Some benchmarks use metrics that only unlock at a certain accuracy level, making capability appear to jump. But the phenomenon of qualitatively new behaviors at scale is real enough that it changed how the field thought about model evaluation.

At 175B parameters, GPT-3 could write coherent financial commentary, generate plausible earnings summaries, perform arithmetic reasoning over balance sheets, and translate between financial jargon and plain language — all from a few examples in the prompt, with no weight updates. From a practical standpoint, this was transformative.

## 4. Why GPT-3 Is Not ChatGPT

GPT-3 was impressive on benchmarks. It was also, in practice, frustrating to use.

Ask it a direct question and it might answer, or it might generate a variant of the question, or it might continue in a completely different direction depending on minute differences in phrasing. Tell it to summarize a document and it might produce a summary, or a critique, or just continue the document. The model had no concept of following instructions — it had only learned to predict what text comes next, and the text that naturally follows a question is not always an answer.

The gap has a name: **alignment**. GPT-3 was trained to model the distribution of internet text. Internet text is not a corpus of helpful assistants answering questions — it's a corpus of human writing in all its variety, including documents where a question is followed by more questions, where requests are followed by refusals, where prompts are followed by tangents.

The model learned what text looks like. It didn't learn what a helpful response looks like.

Fixing this required a different kind of training signal — not raw text prediction, but explicit human feedback about which responses are better. That training process, and the models it produced, is what Part 4 covers.

The distance between GPT-3 and ChatGPT isn't a matter of scale or architecture. It's a matter of whose preferences the model has been trained to reflect.
