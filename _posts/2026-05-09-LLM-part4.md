## 0. The Alignment Problem

A quant fund deploying an LLM for financial research has a specific problem. The model needs to answer questions about balance sheets, summarize earnings calls, and flag risk disclosures. GPT-3 can do all of these — sometimes. But its outputs are erratic. Ask it to summarize a 10-K and it might summarize, or it might critique the filing, or it might continue writing the 10-K itself. Ask it a direct question and it might answer, or it might rephrase the question, or it might generate a list of related questions it found more interesting.

The model isn't broken. It's doing exactly what it was trained to do: generate text that plausibly continues the input. The problem is that "plausible continuation" and "useful response" are different objectives. A GPT-3 prompt is just more text — the model has no privileged concept of a user, a question, or an expected format.

Making the model useful requires training it on a different objective: not "what text is likely to follow this?" but "what response would a knowledgeable, helpful person provide?" These are different distributions. Teaching a model to match the second requires human feedback — labels that tell the model which responses are better, not just which are probable.

This is the alignment problem in its practical form. InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022) solved it with a three-stage pipeline that became the template for ChatGPT and most subsequent instruction-tuned models.

## 1. Supervised Fine-Tuning

The first stage is the simplest: collect demonstrations of ideal model behavior and fine-tune the pre-trained model on them.

OpenAI hired human labelers — domain-knowledgeable contractors — and gave them prompts sampled from actual API usage. For each prompt, the labeler wrote the response they would ideally want the model to produce. This dataset of (prompt, ideal response) pairs was used to fine-tune GPT-3 with a standard supervised cross-entropy objective

$$L_{SFT} = -\sum_{t} \log P_\theta(y_t \mid y_{<t}, x)$$

where $x$ is the prompt and $y$ is the demonstrated response.

SFT substantially improves instruction-following behavior. The model learns the format of helpful responses — answering questions directly, using appropriate structure, avoiding irrelevant tangents. But it has a fundamental limitation.

Human labelers can only demonstrate a finite number of responses. The fine-tuned model learns to imitate those demonstrations, but when it encounters prompts outside the demonstration distribution, it extrapolates in ways that can be harmful, misleading, or simply unhelpful. There's also no mechanism for the model to learn *which* of multiple plausible responses is preferred — SFT provides demonstrations, not rankings.

One thing that confused me was why SFT alone is insufficient, given that the demonstrations are high quality. The issue is distributional: the model generalizes from the demonstrations in ways that can go wrong. A model that has seen thousands of examples of helpful financial explanations might still generate a confidently stated but incorrect earnings figure, because SFT teaches it *how* to explain, not *when* it doesn't actually know. Teaching the model to be calibrated about uncertainty requires a different signal — one that distinguishes better from worse, not just right from wrong format.

## 2. The Reward Model

The second stage addresses what SFT cannot: teaching the model which responses are *better*, not just what responses look like.

Instead of asking labelers to write ideal responses, the second stage asks them to *rank* responses. For each prompt, multiple completions are sampled from the SFT model. Labelers rank these completions from best to worst. This ranking data is used to train a **Reward Model** — a separate neural network that learns to assign a scalar score to any (prompt, response) pair, predicting which responses humans would prefer.

The Reward Model is trained on pairwise comparisons. For a prompt $x$, a preferred response $y_w$ (the winner), and a rejected response $y_l$ (the loser), the loss function pushes the model to assign a higher score to $y_w$ than to $y_l$

$$\mathcal{L}_{RM}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,\, y_w,\, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[\log \sigma\!\left(r_\theta(x, y_w) - r_\theta(x, y_l)\right)\right]$$

The sigmoid function $\sigma$ converts the score difference into a probability: $\sigma(r_\theta(x, y_w) - r_\theta(x, y_l))$ is the probability the model assigns to the correct ranking. $-\log$ of this is the loss — minimized when the model assigns a large positive difference to preferred responses.

Once trained, the Reward Model can score any response to any prompt without human involvement. This is the critical design choice: humans label a finite comparison dataset to train a proxy for their preferences, then that proxy is used at scale during reinforcement learning. The Reward Model is the bottleneck — the quality of alignment depends entirely on whether it has learned to capture what humans actually value.

One thing that confused me was why we train a separate Reward Model at all, rather than simply asking humans to rate every response during training. The answer is compute. PPO generates millions of responses during training and needs to score each one. Human annotation at that scale is infeasible — it would require armies of labelers working in real time. The Reward Model amortizes the human feedback: label a few hundred thousand comparisons once, then use the trained RM to score billions of samples cheaply.

## 3. PPO: Optimizing Against the Reward

With a trained Reward Model in place, the third stage uses reinforcement learning to directly optimize the SFT model to generate responses that score highly. The algorithm is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), applied to the language model as a policy.

The objective has three components

$$\text{objective}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim \pi_\phi^{RL}}\!\left[r_\theta(x, y) - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\phi^{RL}(y \mid x)}{\pi^{SFT}(y \mid x)}\right] + \gamma\, \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}_{pretrain}}\!\left[\log \pi_\phi^{RL}(x)\right]$$

The first term, $r_\theta(x, y)$, maximizes the Reward Model score. This is the primary training signal — the model is rewarded for generating responses the RM considers high quality.

The second term, $-\beta \log(\pi_\phi^{RL} / \pi^{SFT})$, is a KL divergence penalty. It measures how far the RL-trained policy has drifted from the SFT model and subtracts a penalty proportional to that drift. The third term, weighted by $\gamma$, keeps the model performing well on the original pre-training distribution.

The KL penalty deserves close attention because it's doing two things simultaneously. First, it prevents **reward hacking** — the tendency of reinforcement learning policies to find adversarial inputs that exploit gaps in the reward model. Without the penalty, the RL model might learn to generate responses that receive high Reward Model scores while being useless or incoherent in practice. A financial RM trained on a finite comparison dataset has blind spots; a model without a KL constraint will find and exploit them.

Second, the KL penalty prevents **catastrophic forgetting** of the general language capabilities built during pre-training. If the RL objective is optimized aggressively, the model can drift so far from its SFT initialization that it loses coherence outside the fine-tuning distribution. The penalty keeps the RL-trained policy in the neighborhood of the SFT model, where general language quality is preserved.

One thing that confused me was whether the KL penalty means the model can never substantially improve over SFT. It doesn't — the penalty scales with $\beta$, which can be tuned. With small $\beta$, the model has more freedom to deviate; with large $\beta$, it stays close to SFT. In practice, the $\beta$ is small enough to allow substantial improvement on instruction-following while preventing the worst reward hacking failures.

## 4. DPO: Skipping the Reward Model

By 2023, the RLHF pipeline had a clear limitation: it required training and maintaining two separate models — the Reward Model and the policy — with a reinforcement learning training loop between them. RL training is notoriously unstable, sensitive to hyperparameters, and slow. The Reward Model introduces its own failure modes. The entire pipeline is expensive to run and difficult to debug.

Rafailov et al. (2023) asked a different question: does the Reward Model need to exist as a separate model at all? The answer they found was no — under the same optimization objective as RLHF, the language model itself implicitly defines a reward function.

The key insight starts with the RLHF objective. Maximizing expected reward subject to a KL constraint has a closed-form optimal solution

$$\pi^*(y \mid x) \propto \pi^{SFT}(y \mid x) \cdot \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)$$

Rearranging, the reward can be expressed in terms of the policy ratio

$$r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y \mid x)}{\pi^{SFT}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

The partition function $Z(x)$ cancels in pairwise comparisons. Substituting into the pairwise ranking loss yields the DPO objective directly

$$\mathcal{L}_{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\, \pi_{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,\, y_w,\, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}}\!\left[\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_l \mid x)}\right)\right]$$

What makes the DPO loss intuitive is the structure of the argument inside $\sigma$. The term $\beta \log(\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x) / \pi_{ref}(y_w \mid x))$ measures how much more likely the training model is to generate the preferred response compared to the reference — the implicit reward assigned to $y_w$. The second term does the same for $y_l$. The loss maximizes the gap between these implicit rewards, in the direction of human preference.

The language model is not an external judge — it is the reward model. Training it with DPO directly encodes preferences into the model's probability distribution, without the intermediary of a separately trained scorer.

One thing that confused me about DPO is whether removing the Reward Model means losing something fundamental. The RM in RLHF provides a score for any (prompt, response) pair, including pairs that weren't in the training data. DPO has no such explicit scorer — the "reward" is implicit in the policy ratio. In practice, this means DPO can't easily be used to evaluate response quality for arbitrary new prompts during deployment, which is sometimes a useful capability. Whether this matters depends on the application.

DPO doesn't strictly dominate RLHF. For very large models on complex tasks, RLHF with a carefully trained reward model sometimes outperforms DPO, particularly when the reward model is trained on more diverse comparison data than the DPO dataset. For most practical applications at moderate scale, DPO provides competitive alignment performance at a fraction of the engineering complexity.

What the full SFT → RM → PPO → DPO trajectory demonstrates is a recurring pattern in deep learning research: a method that works is followed by a simpler method that achieves the same result with fewer moving parts. The simpler method wins in practice because it's easier to tune, easier to debug, and easier to scale. RLHF solved alignment; DPO made it accessible.
