## 0. The Other Half of the Transformer

GPT's masked self-attention has a hard constraint: when processing position $t$, the model can only attend to positions $1, \ldots, t-1$. This is necessary for generation — if you're predicting the next token, you can't look at it. But for understanding, this constraint is a liability.

Consider a financial sentence: *"Despite the revenue miss, the company's guidance remained [MASK]."* To fill the blank, you need both what came before — *revenue miss, guidance* — and what comes after — *remained* followed by what kind of word? An adjective. Probably positive, given "despite" framing a contrast. The information on both sides of the mask is necessary.

GPT-style models can only use the left context. BERT uses both.

BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) was released by Google in October 2018 — the same year as GPT-1, and roughly the same model size. The architecture is a standard Transformer encoder stack with no masking on the self-attention: every position can attend to every other position freely. The key choices were in how to train it.

## 1. Masked Language Model

GPT's pre-training objective is simple: predict the next token. This forces left-to-right processing, which is exactly the constraint BERT was designed to remove. But if you remove the left-to-right constraint, what do you train on?

The naive answer — predict every token given all other tokens — is circular. If the model sees the whole sequence, there's nothing to predict. BERT's answer is to corrupt the sequence first: randomly replace some tokens with a special `[MASK]` token, then train the model to recover the original tokens. This creates a well-defined prediction task that can be solved only by using context from both directions.

Specifically, 15% of tokens in each sequence are selected for potential replacement. Of those selected tokens, the treatment is not uniform

$$\text{selected token} \rightarrow \begin{cases} \texttt{[MASK]} & \text{with probability 0.80} \\ \text{random token} & \text{with probability 0.10} \\ \text{unchanged} & \text{with probability 0.10} \end{cases}$$

The pre-training loss is computed only over the selected 15% — the model is not penalized for its predictions on the unmasked tokens.

The 80/10/10 split is deliberate. If all selected tokens became `[MASK]`, the model would learn to attend only to non-masked tokens during fine-tuning — since `[MASK]` never appears in real text. The 10% random replacement forces the model to maintain a contextual representation of every token, not just the obviously corrupted ones, because any token could have been silently replaced. The 10% unchanged cases push the model's representations toward actual word identities rather than just "something was here."

For a model trained on financial text, this means the representation of "revenue" in *"The company's revenue [MASK] by 12%"* must encode not just the word itself but also the surrounding context — the fact that something happens to revenue, that 12% is the magnitude, that the company context is positive. That context-sensitivity is exactly what makes BERT representations useful for downstream tasks.

One thing that confused me was why only 15% of tokens are masked, rather than, say, 50%. Masking more creates more training signal per sequence. But it also makes the task harder in a way that hurts convergence — if half the tokens are missing, the remaining context is often insufficient to make confident predictions. 15% seems to be the empirically sweet spot where the task is challenging enough to force useful representations but tractable enough to train stably.

## 2. Next Sentence Prediction

MLM teaches BERT about word-level and phrase-level relationships. But many NLP tasks require understanding relationships between sentences — does this premise support this hypothesis? Does this paragraph follow logically from the previous one? Is this answer relevant to this question?

BERT adds a second pre-training objective — Next Sentence Prediction (NSP) — to address this. During pre-training, each training example consists of two text segments A and B. In 50% of cases, B is the actual sentence that follows A in the original document. In the remaining 50%, B is a randomly selected sentence from a different document.

The model is trained to classify, using a special `[CLS]` token prepended to every input, whether B genuinely follows A

$$\text{input} = \texttt{[CLS]}\; A_1 \ldots A_n\; \texttt{[SEP]}\; B_1 \ldots B_m\; \texttt{[SEP]}$$

For financial NLP, NSP trains the model to understand the logical structure of documents. An earnings call transcript has a coherent narrative arc — results, then discussion, then Q&A. A 10-K has a regulated structure — business overview, risk factors, financials. NSP, in principle, teaches the model to track these narrative relationships rather than treating each sentence in isolation.

In practice, NSP's contribution turned out to be more complicated — but that's worth holding until the end of this post.

## 3. Fine-tuning BERT

BERT's pre-training produces a model with rich bidirectional representations, but it doesn't directly solve any specific task. Fine-tuning adapts the pre-trained weights to a downstream task by adding a small task-specific head on top of the encoder output and training on labeled data.

The mechanism that makes this flexible is the `[CLS]` token. Prepended to every input, it has no intrinsic meaning — its initial embedding is a learned parameter, and after pre-training it doesn't represent any particular word. What it does represent, after passing through all 12 (or 24) encoder layers, is a compressed summary of the entire input. The self-attention in each layer allows `[CLS]` to aggregate information from every other position, making it a natural attachment point for a classification head.

For a financial NLP system, this covers a wide range of tasks from the same pre-trained base.

**Sentiment classification**: Is this earnings announcement positive or negative? Feed the text to BERT, pass `[CLS]` through a linear layer with two outputs, fine-tune on labeled examples.

**Named entity recognition**: Which tokens in this news article are company names, financial figures, or regulatory events? Each token position's output gets a label from a linear classifier.

**Question answering over filings**: Given a 10-K and the question "What was the company's Q3 gross margin?", BERT is fine-tuned to predict the start and end positions of the answer span within the document. The `[SEP]` token separates the question from the passage; the model outputs two probability distributions over token positions.

The practical advantage is that the expensive pre-training step — which requires hundreds of GPU-hours — is done once. Each downstream fine-tuning run is cheap by comparison, typically requiring a few thousand labeled examples and a few minutes of training. This made BERT genuinely accessible to teams without massive compute infrastructure.

One thing that confused me was whether BERT can be used for text generation. It can't — not directly. The encoder produces a rich representation of the input, but it has no autoregressive decoding mechanism. If you want to generate text from a BERT encoder, you need to attach a decoder and define a decoding procedure, at which point you're building something like the original Encoder-Decoder Transformer again. For generation tasks, GPT-style decoder-only models are the natural choice. BERT's strength is in tasks where the full input is available at inference time.

## 4. What RoBERTa Revealed

BERT was a strong empirical result. But it was also under-trained. The original paper used relatively modest training compute — 16 TPUs for 4 days — and made several design choices that turned out to be suboptimal. Facebook's RoBERTa (2019) systematically ablated those choices and found that much of BERT's apparently novel contribution could be squeezed further just by training more carefully.

The changes were not architectural. RoBERTa is the same model as BERT, with the same number of layers, heads, and hidden dimensions. The differences were entirely in the training procedure.

**More data, longer training.** BERT trained on 16GB of text. RoBERTa used 160GB — adding CommonCrawl, CC-News, and OpenWebText on top of BERT's BooksCorpus and Wikipedia. Training ran for longer with larger batch sizes. The result was a substantial performance improvement on every benchmark, suggesting BERT had been substantially under-trained.

**Dynamic masking.** BERT generates its masked inputs once before training begins — the same tokens are masked in every epoch. RoBERTa generates a new mask pattern for each training example in each epoch, giving the model more varied signal from the same data over time.

**Removing NSP.** This was the most surprising finding. RoBERTa dropped Next Sentence Prediction entirely and found that performance on downstream tasks either matched or improved compared to BERT with NSP. Further experiments showed that the apparent benefit of NSP in the original paper came partly from an implementation detail: BERT's NSP training used truncated single sentences as segments, while RoBERTa used full documents. When the comparison was controlled for training data format, NSP contributed nothing.

The NSP finding is worth sitting with. Google published BERT with two pre-training objectives and presented both as contributions. Facebook's ablation study showed that one of those contributions — the one designed specifically to teach sentence-level understanding — was not actually helping. The performance gains attributed to NSP were likely an artifact of confounded experimental design.

One thing that confused me about the NSP failure was why it didn't help, given that tasks like textual entailment and question answering clearly involve understanding sentence relationships. The most plausible explanation is that NSP as implemented was too easy. Distinguishing a sentence that follows a financial earnings report from a randomly selected sentence about a court case requires almost no understanding of sentence relationships — the topic shift is obvious from surface features alone. The model could solve NSP without learning anything about discourse structure.

RoBERTa's lesson is broader than its specific findings: strong baselines require rigorous ablation. It's easy to publish a result that looks like a contribution from a novel training objective when the actual gains come from training longer on more data. The field absorbed this lesson slowly — it remains common for papers to conflate the two.

What BERT and its variants established, collectively, is a template. Pre-train a large encoder on raw text with a self-supervised objective. Fine-tune on small labeled datasets for specific tasks. The representations learned during pre-training transfer remarkably well across domains — a model trained on Wikipedia and books can be fine-tuned for financial sentiment analysis, medical record classification, or legal document understanding with modest amounts of domain-specific labeled data. That transferability is BERT's lasting contribution, independent of the specific training objectives that produced it.
